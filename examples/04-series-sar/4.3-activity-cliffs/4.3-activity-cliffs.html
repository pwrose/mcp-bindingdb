👤 User

Find the activity cliffs on menin, but only count a pair when both compounds were measured in the same patent — I don't want to compare two labs' assays. Check the assay conditions where they are recorded.

🧠 Assistant

Among the 928 compounds with an exact IC50 against human menin (target 6958, O00255), activity_cliffs finds 273 pairs at Tanimoto ≥ 0.85 that differ by at least 100-fold. Reading each pair inside a single document instead of across the database changes the answer substantially:

After checking the source of both values Pairs
One document reports both compounds, and the gap is still ≥ 100-fold there 184
One document reports both, but the gap shrinks below 100-fold when read there 50
No document reports both — the two values come from different places 39

So a third of the list does not survive the rule, and the survivors are not spread evenly: 169 of the 184 sit inside one patent and 15 inside one paper. By document: US12473295B2 98, US10899738B2 51, US20240335431A1 16, US11045448B2 4, and the papers PMID 23244744 10 and PMID 31244110 5.

The 89 that were thrown out have a single cause: 85 of them are one disagreement between two documents of the same patent family. Three entries are the entire contribution of US20240261292A1 (2024) to this target, reported there at 0.09, 0.098 and 0.18 nM; two of them reappear in US12473295B2 (2025) at 80 and 90 nM, 890-fold and 500-fold weaker, and the third is a duplicate entry whose SMILES is character-for-character identical to one of the others. Both documents give the same written protocol, so nothing in the recorded conditions explains the gap, and the rule is right to refuse the comparison.

Why the rule is not the same as what the tool reports. activity_cliffs ranks each compound by its most potent exact value anywhere in the database, which is the right default — but it means the two halves of a pair can come from two different labs. Here that is not a hypothetical: the pair at the top of the raw output is 28,000-fold and its two values come from two different patents. The check is one SQL query: list every (compound, source) value for the compounds in the cliff list, keep the pairs with a source in common, and recompute the difference from that source's own two numbers.

What the conditions say. For the two pairs in the figure the check is clean, and it is worth being precise about what "checked" means here:

  • Both values of each pair carry the same assay_name, because each pair sits in one patent and each of these patents reports everything under one assay.
  • The ph and temp_k columns are empty — for all 988 menin IC50 rows, not just these.
  • The conditions are nonetheless recorded, in the assay table's protocol text: US12473295B2 runs a terbium-labelled menin / FITC-MBM1 peptide HTRF competition in 40 mM Tris·HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.05% Pluronic F-127, 30 minutes at ambient temperature; US11045448B2 runs a fluorescence polarisation competition with a FAM-labelled MLL1 peptide. Different formats, which is exactly why a cliff should not straddle them.

The survivors, and what they are made of. 40 of the 184 have identical fingerprints — sali is null because the structural distance is zero — and on this target that almost always means a configuration: 16 of them in US10899738B2, 12 in US20240335431A1, 10 in US12473295B2, 2 in US11045448B2. The rest are small constitutional changes. The figure shows one of each kind.

  • A separated pair of isomers, 2 nM against 2,200 nM in US11045448B2 — same molecule, same assay, same table, inverted at the carbon that carries the nitrile. A 1,100-fold eutomer/distomer ratio is real SAR and is the kind of cliff worth having.
  • A tail group, 65 nM against 88,840 nM in US12473295B2 — the basic amine at the end of the chain replaced by a short alcohol, 1,400-fold. The length-matched version of that swap is in the same patent: its 3-hydroxypropyl analogue is 70,390 nM, so the result is not an artefact of the shorter chain. On this chemotype the basic nitrogen is worth about three logs.

US10899738B2 makes the stereochemical point repeatedly: its cyclopentyl esters are reported as separated (1S,2R) and (1R,2S) forms, and across the series the first is 500–1,000-fold the more potent — 13 against 13,800 nM for the acetate, 237 against 164,600 nM for the isobutyrate.

Two things the rule does not fix. First, nearly every value here is a single measurement, so "same patent" removes the cross-lab problem but not a single mistyped number. Second, the largest surviving cliff — 16 nM against 923,130 nM in US10899738B2 — changes two things at once, the aryl group (3-fluorophenyl to 3-trifluoromethylphenyl) as well as the configuration, so it is not a clean one-change pair however well measured it is. Cliffs at the top of a list are worth reading individually before they are worth believing.

How this was answered

  1. search_targets for "menin" in Homo sapiens gave target 6958 (O00255, 1,073 compounds); the tool picked IC50, its most measured exact type.
  2. activity_cliffs with min_similarity: 0.85, min_delta_p: 2.0 and max_compounds: 2000 returned all 273 pairs, each with both compounds' ids and best values.
  3. run_sql listed every (compound, source_id) value for the 213 compounds appearing in those pairs; a pair was kept only where one source_id reports both, and the difference was recomputed from that source's two values.
  4. run_sql read assay_name, ph and temp_k for the figure's four compounds, and joined activity to the assay table on entryid for the protocol text behind each patent.

👤 User

How much does BindingDB actually record about the conditions? Can I always do this?

🧠 Assistant

Enough to do this on most targets, as long as you accept that "the conditions" usually means a block of prose rather than two numeric columns. Over every exact Ki, Kd, IC50 and EC50 value in nM:

Source Values Sources pH recorded Temperature recorded Assay name
Article (PMID) 1,488,682 45,181 2.9% 2.6% 98.5%
Patent 1,030,639 8,679 13.4% 12.6% 98.9%
PubChem assay 72,558 663 3.8% 5.8% 92.9%

So the numeric columns are the exception. ph and temp_k are populated for roughly one value in ten from patents and one in thirty from papers — and, against intuition, patents are the better-curated source for them, not the worse. On menin they are empty for all 988 rows, which is why the previous answers leaned on the assay text instead: US12473295B2 states pH 7.5 in its protocol, and that pH never reaches the ph column.

What is almost always there is assay_name — on 98–99% of values — and behind it the assay table, keyed by entryid, which carries the full protocol paragraph: buffer, probe, peptide, incubation, plate format, often the temperature in words ("ambient"). That is the layer to read when a comparison matters. activity joins to it on entryid, and one query per document is enough.

"Same patent" is a good proxy for "same assay", but not a perfect one. Of the 8,679 patents with exact values, 6,969 report everything under a single assay name; 1,256 use two, 272 three, 107 four, 30 five, and 14 have none recorded. So about one patent in five mixes formats — typically a biochemical assay and a cell assay in the same document, whose IC50s belong in different columns entirely. The robust rule is therefore two conditions, not one: same source_id and same assay_name. On menin that distinction changes nothing, because each of the relevant patents uses one name; on a patent with a biochemical and a cellular readout it changes everything.

What this never gives you, on any target. The same patent is not the same plate, the same day or the same protein batch — a patent's table is often assembled over several years of a programme. And a document reporting one assay name can still report values measured in two different laboratories. Within-document comparison removes the largest and most systematic source of noise; it does not turn a database into a single experiment.

A practical order to check things in, cheapest first:

  1. source_id — do both numbers come from one document at all? (One SQL query, as in the first answer.)
  2. assay_name — is it the same assay within that document?
  3. The assay table's description — what was actually done, and does the text name a pH, a temperature, a probe concentration?
  4. ph and temp_k — use them when present; do not design a workflow that depends on them.
  5. The number of measurements behind each side — n_measurements in the SAR tools, or get_activity for the individual rows. A cliff where both sides are single values is a hypothesis, however well sourced.

How this was answered

  1. run_sql grouped every exact Ki/Kd/IC50/EC50 value in nM by the kind of source its source_id denotes, counting how often ph, temp_k and assay_name are populated.
  2. run_sql counted distinct assay_name values per patent, over all patents with exact measurements.
  3. The menin figure in the previous answer came from the same two tables: activity for the values and their assay_name, the assay table for the protocol text behind each entryid.
