Single-atom tweaks are not where my project is. On factor Xa, which whole-group swaps have a track record of improving potency?

🧠 Assistant

Among the 1,500 most potent compounds with an exact Ki against human factor Xa (target 1341, P00742), matched_molecular_pairs finds 9,442 pairs over 7,387 distinct transformations. Keeping only the changes that swap a group of four or more heavy atoms for another leaves 264 of the 500 most frequent — and unlike the single-atom tweaks, several of them have a real record.

Compare that with the single-atom changes on the same target, which are far more common and almost inert: hydrogen → methyl 119 pairs, median 0.00, 53 improved; hydrogen → fluorine 69 pairs, +0.02, 36 improved; hydrogen → chlorine 35 pairs, +0.19; methyl → trifluoromethyl 28 pairs, +0.20. A group swap is made a tenth as often and is worth reading one by one, because when it moves potency it moves it by a log unit, not by a rounding error.

The top row is the S1 story of this whole target class. Factor Xa's S1 pocket was addressed for years with a basic benzamidine; the programmes that produced oral drugs replaced it with neutral aryls, and the 3-amino-1,2-benzisoxazole is the one that kept the hydrogen-bonding pattern without the charge. In these pairs it beats a 4-methoxyphenyl in 13 of 14 matched compounds, by 8-fold at the median and 44-fold at best (6.9 → 0.16 nM). The figure shows the swap in two different scaffolds: an N-arylpyrazole carboxamide where it gives 3.5 → 0.09 nM (razaxaban's chemotype) and the bicyclic pyrazolopyridinone where it gives 0.18 → 0.03 nM (BMS-740808). The 14 pairs come from about a dozen publications, so this is one long programme seen from many papers rather than one table copied twice.

The second row is the P4 lactam, and it is the same kind of result: flattening the saturated 2-oxopiperidine to an aromatic 2-oxopyridinone improved 11 of 12 pairs, median 5-fold. The cleanest pair in that set is a chloroindole carboxamide, 2.6 nM with the saturated lactam against 0.67 nM with the pyridinone.

And the drug does neither. Apixaban (BDBM19023, factor Xa Ki 0.075 nM, 15 exact values) carries both left-hand groups: the 4-methoxyphenyl in S1 and the 2-oxopiperidin-1-yl in P4. On this evidence each could have been swapped for something more potent. It was not, and that is the right reading of a matched-pair table: at 0.075 nM and 40,000-fold selectivity over thrombin, potency had stopped being the problem, and a neutral, metabolically plain methoxyphenyl is easier to dose than an aminoisoxazole. Matched pairs rank changes on the one axis the database holds.

What the negative rows are worth. Pyrrolidine → morpholine lost potency in 10 of 11 pairs (median −0.43). That is a solubility move being paid for in affinity, visible here because somebody made both compounds; so is methanesulfonyl → sulfamoyl (6 of 22 improved). These are the rows a project actually needs: not "what helps" but "what this target will tolerate".

How this was answered

  1. search_targets for "coagulation factor X" in Homo sapiens gave target 1341 (P00742, 7,201 compounds); the tool picked Ki, its most measured exact type.
  2. matched_molecular_pairs with group_by_transformation: true, max_compounds: 1500 and limit: 500 returned one row per transformation with its pair count, median, best and worst change.
  3. Those rows were filtered to the ones whose two fragments both have at least four heavy atoms — the tool reports every transformation, including the single-atom ones, so "a group, not an atom" is a filter on the output.
  4. matched_molecular_pairs with transformation set to each of the three interesting swaps returned the individual pairs behind them, and draw_structure_grid drew two of the benzisoxazole pairs.

👤 User

The benzisoxazole is the neutral P1 that made oral factor Xa inhibitors possible. Did the same move work on thrombin, where the P1 was a benzamidine?

🧠 Assistant

Not with that group — the 3-aminobenzisoxazole does not appear in thrombin's pairs at all — but the same move does, with a different group, and it worked: a para-benzamidine replaced by a neutral 2-(aminomethyl)-5-chlorophenyl improved 7 of 8 matched pairs, 2-fold at the median and 8-fold at best. And the two swaps with better records than that are both further work on the group that replaced it.

The P1 row answers the question: the basic amidine was not load-bearing. The figure below shows two pairs from the same pyridinone/pyrazinone series, one with a benzylsulfonamide and one with a phenethylamino group, each measured in both P1 forms. The benzamidine is 0.37 nM and the chlorobenzylamine 0.046 nM — 8-fold better without the charge; the second pair moves 0.57 → 0.13 nM. A third pair elsewhere in the set goes 0.102 → 0.052 nM, and only one of the eight goes backwards (−0.48, a D-Phe-Pro peptidomimetic). The amine is still present, but it has moved off the ring and out of the pocket, and a chlorine now sits in thrombin's hydrophobic S1. That is the same structural bet the factor Xa programmes made, reached from a different chemotype — and here the neutral group was not even a potency sacrifice.

The top two rows say which part of the new group is doing the work, and it is the chlorine. Both act on that same replacement benzyl ring. Adding the chlorine while switching the azole back from tetrazole to triazole improved all 8 of its pairs, median 11-fold, worst case still +0.26 — the only row in either target's table with no losers. Keeping the chlorine and changing the azole the other way, triazole → tetrazole, is worth 5-fold over 14 pairs. Read together: the chlorine in S1 is the large effect, the azole next to it is a smaller one, and the amidine the series started with was worth less than either.

What the comparison is worth, and where it stops. Eight pairs is a thin record next to factor Xa's fourteen, and all eight come from one programme, so this is one laboratory's result rather than a cross-programme consensus. More importantly, the database cannot show you why either swap mattered: replacing a benzamidine was never about affinity, it was that a permanently charged group does not cross a gut wall. Matched pairs measure the axis BindingDB holds. The useful finding here is the negative one — the project could drop the charge without paying for it.

The bottom two rows are the ones to show someone who likes saturated rings. Cyclohexyl → phenyl is a coin flip (7 of 18 pairs improved, median −0.05) and trans-4-aminocyclohexyl → aminopyridine loses more often than it wins (7 of 15, median −0.23, worst −3.56), even though both are routine moves for lowering logP. Note how wide those rows are: ±1.5 log units around a median of roughly zero means the change is not neutral, it is context-dependent, and the median is hiding two populations. That is the one place a matched-pair median actively misleads, and the fix is to open the row and look at the pairs.

👤 User

Why does it only find groups up to a certain size, and what happens if I allow bigger ones?

🧠 Assistant

Two rules decide it, and only one of them is a setting you can change.

The setting is max_variable_atoms, which defaults to 13 heavy atoms. It caps the size of the part that changes. Raising it does find more:

max_variable_atoms Pairs Transformations Of the 500 most frequent, swaps of 10+ atoms
8 5,616 3,984 0
13 (default) 9,442 7,387 50
20 14,427 12,063 135
25 14,503 12,139 135

The other rule is the one that stops the table growing: the part that changes may never be larger than the part that stays. That is what makes a pair a pair — two compounds with a common core and one different substituent. It is why 25 adds 76 pairs to 20 and no new transformations worth the name: by then the limit is not the setting, it is that a 24-atom "substituent" would have to hang off a core of at least 24 atoms, and few such compounds exist in this set. A third, quieter rule works the same way: a pair is found by cutting one acyclic single bond, so rings are never opened. A scaffold hop — pyrazole to pyrazolopyridinone, say — will never show up here no matter how high you set the limit, because the change cannot be written as one substituent swap.

What raising it actually buys is changes to rings in the middle of a molecule — at the price of generality. At 20 atoms the biggest new row is a benzenesulfonamide-biphenyl whose far ring changes from a phenyl to a pyridine: 13 pairs, median +0.25. That ring is internal, so no single cut can isolate it; the only way to express the change is to carry the sulfonamide and the second ring along with it. The default limit cannot see it at all. But look at what else appears alongside it: ten more rows describing the same small family of distal-ring changes — phenyl, pyridyl, pyrimidinyl, fluorophenyl — in different amounts of context, at 9, 7, 6, 4, 4, 4, 3, 3, 2 and 2 pairs. Meanwhile the context-free row that says plain phenyl → pyridyl has 3 pairs, at both limits, because it only applies where that ring is terminal. So one chemical change arrives as a dozen rows that you have to recognise as the same thing, and no row states the general case. The row count doubles, the per-row statistics thin out, and the run takes longer, because every extra fragment is another key to index.

So the practical answer is: leave it at 13 unless you know you are after a change to a ring in the middle of the molecule. The two settings worth moving are the other two:

  • max_compounds (1,500 here) decides how much of the target you pair at all; this target has 7,201 compounds, so the table above is built from the most potent fifth of them. Raising it finds rarer transformations and costs time.
  • transformation, once you have a row you care about, which switches the tool from the summary table to the individual pairs behind it. That is the call that produced both figures in this example.

And if you genuinely want the large-fragment view — "what else has anyone put in this pocket?" — matched pairs is the wrong tool for it. decompose_r_groups with a core you specify will take substituents of any size, because you have told it where the core ends instead of asking it to guess.

How this was answered

  1. matched_molecular_pairs was run four times on factor Xa (target 1341) with max_variable_atoms at 8, 13, 20 and 25, everything else unchanged.
  2. The transformation rows from each run were filtered to the ones changing 10 or more heavy atoms on both sides, to count what each limit actually bought.
  3. The new rows at 20 and 25 were read individually, and compared with the minimal-fragment row for the same change, to see whether the bigger limit found new chemistry or re-described what the default already had.
