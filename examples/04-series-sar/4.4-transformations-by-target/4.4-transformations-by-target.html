👤 User

Is the fluorine effect target-specific? Compare what adding a fluorine, a methyl and a chlorine do across a kinase, a protease, a hydrolase, a metalloenzyme, a GPCR, an ion channel and a nuclear receptor — and only count a pair when both compounds were measured in the same patent or paper.

🧠 Assistant

No — and the rule does not change that answer, which is itself worth knowing. Across a kinase, a protease, a hydrolase, a metalloenzyme, a GPCR, an ion channel and a nuclear receptor, these three changes make 1,684 matched pairs, and 1,582 of them (94%) have a patent or paper that reports both compounds under one assay name. Read inside those documents, a fluorine and a methyl still do essentially nothing at the median, everywhere; a chlorine does one specific thing on one target.

The flat result is robust. Across all seven targets the fluorine median moves from 0.00 to +0.01 when the rule is applied, and the methyl median stays at 0.00. The seven-target band is a third of a log unit wide either way. Whatever a fluorine is worth in your series, the protein class does not predict it, and this is not an artefact of mixing laboratories — it survives when every pair is read from one table.

What the rule changes is the tails, not the middle. Counting pairs that move potency by more than tenfold in either direction:

Pairs As the database reports them Read inside one document
Fluorine 636 → 587 checked 29 14
Methyl 744 → 709 37 29
Chlorine 304 → 286 41 39

Half the dramatic fluorine results disappear, and the worst of them go first: the most negative fluorine pair in the whole set, −3.05, a fluorine apparently costing a thousandfold, was one compound's best value in one paper set against another compound's best value in another. Read inside documents, nothing below −1.48 survives. The discarded pairs are not a random sample either — they lean positive (median +0.16, 67% improved, against +0.01 and 51% for the checked ones) and 6 of the 29 tenfold-plus fluorine moves sit in the 8% of pairs the rule throws away.

AChE is where this bites. It is the only target where the check fails often: 10 of its 36 fluorine pairs have no shared document, and those 10 carry 5 of the 11 tenfold-plus moves. Its ligands come almost entirely from small academic papers (only one of its 125 checked fluorine and methyl pairs sits in a patent), and the same compound measured in two of those papers is the classic source of a spurious effect. After the check its fluorine median rises from +0.08 to +0.21 on 26 pairs — a cleaner number, but one you should not lean on.

The chlorine column is the finding that survives everything. On hERG, 29 of 31 chlorine pairs are single-document, and 93% of them show stronger block, median +0.56 (3.6-fold), maximum +2.12. For hERG "stronger" is worse: this is the antitarget, and a chlorine reliably makes it worse while a fluorine on the same channel does nothing (median 0.00 over 89 pairs). That is the lipophilicity story in one row — a chlorine adds roughly 0.7 to logP, a fluorine about 0.14 — and it is now measured inside single documents rather than across them. AChE's chlorine behaves the same way (+0.30 over 107 pairs, 71% improved), which is a real series effect in its bivalent inhibitors rather than a class rule: the other five targets sit between −0.11 and +0.05.

Two honest notes on the method.

  • Reading inside one document does not always shrink a difference. For hERG's fluorines the largest within-document move (+3.46) is bigger than anything the database-wide comparison showed, and CA II's chlorine goes from 3 tenfold-plus moves to 6. The rule changes which two numbers you compare; it is not a smoothing filter.
  • Where several documents report both compounds, this takes the middle document's answer, not the most dramatic one. For an effect size that is the right choice; for hunting cliffs it would not be.

How this was answered

  1. matched_molecular_pairs was called for each of the seven targets with transformation set to [H][*:1] >> F[*:1], [H][*:1] >> C[*:1] and [H][*:1] >> Cl[*:1], max_compounds: 1500 — 21 calls, each returning the individual pairs with both compounds' ids and best values.
  2. For each target, run_sql listed every (compound, document, assay name) value behind those pairs. A pair counts only when one document reports both compounds under one assay name, and its change is recomputed from that document's own two numbers.
  3. The 1,684 pairs, with the document and assay behind each and both versions of its delta, are in transformation_deltas.csv; plot_transformation_effects.py draws the figure from that file and rebuilds it from the database when it is missing.

👤 User

What did the rule cost me on each target, and which of these targets can I trust it on at all?

🧠 Assistant

It cost very little almost everywhere, and a lot on exactly one target. Of the 1,684 pairs, 102 had no document reporting both compounds under one assay — the grey dots in the figure above — and 42 of those 102 belong to AChE.

ROR-γ 252 249 (99%) 33 · 214 patent, 35 article US9663502B2, 61 pairs

hERG 246 232 (94%) 45 · 122 article, 110 patent US9981950B2, 48 pairs

The split by assay name inside a document cost almost nothing — 12 pairs in total, out of 1,594 that share a document. Patents and papers that report two analogues usually report them in the same table under one protocol; it is worth insisting on, but it is not where the losses are.

Three targets you can lean on. Adenosine A2A (235 of 236), ROR-γ (249 of 252) and CA II (161 of 167) lose essentially nothing, and all three give a flat answer for every substituent: nine medians, all between −0.05 and +0.05. Those flat numbers are now worth something, because they are not flat merely from mixing laboratories.

AChE is the one to distrust, and it is the one with the most interesting-looking result. Its fluorine pairs are only 72% checkable (26 of 36), and the 10 discarded pairs carry 5 of the 11 tenfold-plus moves; its methyl loses 19 of 118 and its chlorine 13 of 120. Its ligands come from the academic literature almost exclusively — 225 of its 232 checked pairs sit in a paper, and only one of its 125 checked fluorine and methyl pairs in a patent — and they are spread over 60 documents, none contributing more than 24 pairs, so a matched pair often links two different research groups' compounds. Its chlorine signal (+0.30 over 107 pairs, 71% improved) survives the check, but 26 of those pairs still move potency more than tenfold: that is a target where a chlorine sometimes matters enormously and sometimes not at all, not a target with a reliable chlorine increment. Being article-based is not itself the problem — CA II is 100% article and 96% checkable. The problem is a crowded literature of close analogues published separately.

Two targets that are checkable but not independent. BACE1 keeps 93% of its pairs, but 141 of its 317 come from one patent's FRET assay, and EGFR has 61 of 156 in one patent's high-throughput assay. Across all seven targets the 1,582 checked pairs rest on 338 documents and 361 document-and-assay combinations, and the five biggest of those carry 341 pairs between them. The rule guarantees the two numbers in a pair are comparable; it does not guarantee that 300 pairs are 300 independent observations. For BACE1 the fluorine result (+0.09, 62% improved, 192 pairs) is largely one company's series, so read it as "a fluorine helped in this chemotype" rather than as a property of aspartyl proteases.

What the rule actually changed. Nowhere did a median move by more than 0.13 log units — AChE's fluorine (+0.08 → +0.21) is the largest shift on the board, BACE1's methyl (−0.11 → −0.02) the next. The rule buys confidence and tail discipline, not a different central answer: tenfold-plus moves fall from 11 to 4 on AChE's fluorine, 15 to 9 on its methyl, 32 to 26 on its chlorine, 2 to 0 on EGFR's fluorine. On CA II's chlorine it goes the other way, 3 to 6, because four of the within-document comparisons are sharper than the cross-document ones they replace.
