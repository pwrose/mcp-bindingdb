👤 User

Find triples of compounds with the following properties that may have swapped parts: (1) same target; (2) the first compound is made up of two fragments, A-B; (3) the second compound is made up of two fragments, C-D; (4) the third compound is made of A-C, B-C, A-D or B-D; (5) each fragment must be at least 1/3 of the whole molecule.

🧠 Assistant

Here is how that maps onto what BindingDB can answer.

The third compound shares a fragment with each of the other two. So it forms a matched pair with the first compound, since both contain the fragment they have in common, and another matched pair with the second. A swap triple is two matched molecular pairs that share a compound, with the extra condition that the two pairs swap different halves. That means the same machinery behind matched_molecular_pairs finds them: cut each ligand at one acyclic single bond, index the fragments, and look for a compound whose two halves come from two different parents.

Three conditions decide what counts:

  • The 1/3 rule. A cut is kept only when each half holds at least a third of the molecule's heavy atoms. This is what makes it a swap of substantial parts rather than a substituent change, and it is stricter than the server's own matched-pair rule, which only requires the changed group to be no larger than the constant part.
  • Four distinct fragments. A, B, C and D must all differ, otherwise the "swap" is an ordinary matched pair.
  • Three distinct constitutions. Two epimers fragment into halves that differ only in stereochemistry, so without this test an epimer pair looks like a swap of two different fragments. Requiring the three compounds to differ once stereochemistry is removed drops those.

One more thing is worth recording whenever it is available: the fourth corner. If A-B and C-D give the hybrid A-C, then B-D is the remaining recombination, and when it has been measured too the square closes. That turns the triple into a double-mutant cycle, where the two halves' contributions can be added up and compared with what the hybrid actually does.

The search runs per target, since the fragment index is built from one target's ligands.

Try HIV-1 protease.

🧠 Assistant

HIV-1 protease is a good choice for this: its ligands are famously modular, built from a transition-state core with a P2 cap on one side and a P2' group on the other, and a great many of them have been measured.

What the search found

The target is Gag-Pol polyprotein [489-587] (polymerid 223), BindingDB's main HIV-1 protease entry, with 3,548 compounds carrying a measured affinity. Of those, 2,951 split into two fragments that are each at least a third of the molecule.

Those ligands yield 537,744 raw triples, which badly overstates the chemistry. Only 1,250 distinct compounds ever serve as the hybrid, and a handful of fragments dominate: the Boc-phenylalanine halves and the darunavir-style 4-methoxyphenylsulfonamide half each appear in tens of thousands of triples, because they recur across hundreds of papers. Collapsing to one representative per swap axis - a hybrid together with the two fragments it is cut into - and then to one per distinct compound triple leaves 1,666 triples. Of these:

  • 1,531 are comparable, meaning all three compounds have the same affinity type
  • 1,167 have a paper or patent reporting all three compounds
  • 1,162 close the square, and 1,067 of those have a value for the fourth corner as well

As a check, rejoining the two kept fragments reproduces the hybrid's structure in all 1,666 rows.

Does recombining halves help?

Usually not, and the first figure below gives the distribution. Among triples whose three values come from one document, the hybrid is a median 0.75 log units worse than its better parent, and it beats both parents only 25% of the time. That is the honest base rate, and it is the reason a hybrid worth looking at has to be picked rather than assumed.

The second panel of that figure is the more interesting one. For the squares that close, the hybrid lands a median 0.07 log units from the sum of the two halves' separate effects, so fragment contributions are additive on average. But 23% of them miss that additive expectation by more than tenfold in one direction or the other. Additivity is a good default and a bad guarantee.

Two squares worth looking at

The second figure is a case where recombination works. In a 2020 sulfonamide series the molecules divide into a P2 urea-or-glycinamide half and an arylsulfonamide half. Neither parent is impressive at 17 nM and 295 nM, and the fourth corner sits at 126 nM, yet the hybrid that takes the morpholine urea from one and the 4-methoxyphenylsulfonamide from the other reaches 0.21 nM, about 80 times better than its better parent and roughly 2.3 log units beyond what adding the two halves' effects would predict. This is the pattern that makes the search worth running: the good combination was not visible in either parent.

The third figure is the opposite, and it is the more instructive one. In the KNI peptidomimetic series three of the four corners are subnanomolar, but the A-C hybrid, KNI-1278, falls to 5 micromolar - more than four orders of magnitude, and 3.5 log units below the additive prediction. The reason is visible in the structure: that particular recombination is the one that keeps the shorter cap from one parent while dropping the other's P3 extension, so the hybrid is simply much smaller than the other three. A swap that looks symmetric on paper is not symmetric in the binding site.

Caveats

The triples span many papers and assay conditions, so a comparison drawn across documents is weaker evidence than one within a single paper. The table carries a shared_article flag, and the figure separates the two; I would treat the cross-document rows as leads rather than measurements. Affinities are the median per compound per type, and Ki and IC50 are never mixed within a triple.

The ligand set is also not neutral chemistry. These series were designed around a common core, so the fragments that recur most are the ones medicinal chemists deliberately reused, and the triples are correspondingly enriched in planned analogues rather than accidental ones.
