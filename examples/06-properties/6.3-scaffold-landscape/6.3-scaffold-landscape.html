The map is readable in a way a target's chemical space usually is not: the coloured islands are dense and far apart, and even the gray remainder breaks into small clusters rather than scattering. The reason is worth knowing before you generalise from it. PDE2A is a young CNS target whose chemistry comes almost entirely from patent series: a single patent contributes 50–100 close analogues at once, each a small variation on one core. A target with a long literature history looks nothing like this. The same query on four other targets:

A lesson about Murcko scaffolds and ring substituents. The carboxamide family is one chemical series by any chemist's reading, but group_by_scaffold returns it as 10 separate scaffolds. The cause is a rule, not a bug: a Bemis-Murcko scaffold keeps every ring system, so a phenyl or pyrimidinyl at position 2 becomes part of the scaffold, while a methyl or a dimethylamino at the same position disappears. Whether two analogues land in the same group depends on whether the group you varied happens to be a ring. Grouping by generic_scaffold (107 groups instead of 170) merges the 2-phenyl and 2-pyrimidinyl variants, since both reduce to the same carbon skeleton, but still separates the 2-benzyl one, which has an extra atom in the linker. For a question like "how large is this series", read the scaffold groups together with the map, or match the core with substructure_search.

A lesson about target names in the PDE family. BindingDB calls PDE2A "cGMP-dependent 3',5'-cyclic phosphodiesterase" and PDE5A "cGMP-specific 3',5'-cyclic phosphodiesterase" — one word apart, and neither contains "PDE2" or "PDE5". search_targets resolves the gene name, but it is worth confirming the UniProt accession (O00408 for PDE2A, O76074 for PDE5A) before running an analysis. The isoform and construct entries (PDE2A1, PDE2A2, and the [574-941] catalytic domain) are separate targets with the same accession and only a handful of compounds each.

How this was answered

  1. search_targets for "PDE2A" gave target 788 (O00408, 3,047 compounds).
  2. find_ligands_for_target with max_value_nm: 10 and group_by_scaffold: true grouped the potent ligands by the scaffold column of compound_property, which holds RDKit's Bemis-Murcko scaffold of each parent structure; the same call with generic_scaffold: true gave the carbon-skeleton grouping.
  3. A run_sql query listed every scaffold in the carboxamide family with its generic scaffold, to see which variants merge.
  4. A second query compared the concentration of potent chemistry across five targets.
  5. The compounds and their scaffolds were exported with run_sql, fingerprinted with RDKit, laid out with scikit-learn's t-SNE on Jaccard distances, and added as Figure 1 with add_figure.

Show me one compound from each family with its potency.

🧠 Assistant

Figure 2 shows one compound from each family, with BAY 60-7550 for reference. They share no common core, which is why they occupy four separate territories in Figure 1: the only substructure all five contain is a benzene ring, and that is what the grid has aligned and highlighted.

What the four families have in common is not a scaffold. Each carries a flat, electron-poor heterocycle with a lactam or amide carbonyl — the part that hydrogen-bonds to the invariant glutamine of the PDE active site — and hangs a lipophilic, usually fluorinated aryl group off it. The pharmacophore is conserved; the ring system carrying it is not. That is the pattern a scaffold grouping cannot see and a fingerprint map can: four islands, one recurring binding motif.

Potency and evidence. The four patent families reach sub-nanomolar potency (best pAffinity 9.3 to 10.8 in the scaffold table above), and the representatives in Figure 2 are at or below 1 nM. Each rests on one or two exact measurements, from the patent that reported it — a patent example is typically measured once, so the bars are thin evidence by construction. BAY 60-7550 is the exception in every way: it is the only compound in the entire potent set with a name rather than a patent label or a ChEMBL id, it has 16 exact PDE2A values with a median of 4.7 nM, and it is the weakest of the five.

A lesson about what BindingDB holds for a young target. 582 of PDE2A's 950 potent compounds come from four families of patents filed between 2013 and 2019. That is where this target's chemistry lives, and BindingDB curates it — which is why the landscape looks the way it does. Two consequences for analysing any target like this:

  • Compound names are not names. "US10285989, Example 14" tells you the patent, not what anyone calls the molecule, and a search by drug name finds nothing at all.
  • Measurement counts are not evidence weight in the usual sense. One hundred compounds measured once each, in one assay, in one laboratory, is not the same body of evidence as twenty compounds measured in twenty laboratories. Both are rows in activity.

How this was answered

  1. A run_sql query took the SMILES, heavy-atom count, cLogP and median exact PDE2A value of one representative per family — the most potent compound of each family's largest scaffold — plus BAY 60-7550.
  2. draw_structure_grid drew them without align_to. With no core to pass, it falls back to the largest common substructure, which for these five is a single benzene ring — a fair summary of how little they share.
