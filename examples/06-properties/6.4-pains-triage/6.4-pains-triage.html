👤 User

5-lipoxygenase has a long literature of natural-product inhibitors and I don't trust much of it. How many of its reported inhibitors carry PAINS alerts, which alerts, and does that track with potency?

🧠 Assistant

A lot of them, and the alerts all tell the same story. But the alert does not track potency, so the alert count on its own will not triage this target.

How many

Of the 2,411 ALOX5 compounds (polymerid 2259, P09917) with an exact = IC50/Ki/Kd/EC50 value, 416 carry at least one PAINS alert — 17.3%. The database-wide rate is 61,867 of 1,464,874 compounds, 4.22%. 5-LOX is enriched about four-fold.

-- database-wide baseline
SELECT COUNT(*) AS n_cpd,
       SUM(CASE WHEN len(pains) > 0 THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS n_pains
FROM compound_property;

Note that compound_property.pains is an empty list, not NULL, for a compound with no alert — WHERE pains IS NOT NULL silently matches everything. Use len(pains) > 0.

This is not an assortment of unrelated filters. Catechols, quinones, rhodanine-like enes, imine-ones, Mannich bases, azo dyes — that is the redox-active half of the PAINS catalogue, almost to the exclusion of everything else.

That is the part worth pausing on, because it is mechanistically specific to this target. 5-LOX is a non-heme iron dioxygenase: the resting enzyme is Fe(III) and has to be oxidised to turn over. The standard assay reads the conjugated-diene product by absorbance around 234 nm, or measures 5-HETE/LTB4 formation. A compound that reduces Fe(III) to Fe(II) switches the enzyme off without occupying any pocket, and a coloured or UV-absorbing compound interferes with the readout directly.

So the alert list is, in effect, enumerating the two ways you can get a potent-looking number out of this assay without having an inhibitor. That is a far more useful thing to know than "17% are flagged".

Does it track potency?

No.

best measured value compounds with a PAINS alert %
≤ 10 nM 42 5 11.9
10–100 nM 415 54 13.0
0.1–1 µM 958 197 20.6
> 1 µM 996 160 16.1

If PAINS alerts marked artefactually potent compounds, the flagged fraction would climb towards the potent end. It does the opposite: the peak is in the 0.1–1 µM band, and the most potent band is the least flagged of all four. Flagged compounds are spread through the whole dataset rather than concentrated at the top.

Promiscuity does not separate them either. Among the 1,415 compounds with a ≤1 µM value, the 256 flagged ones are measured against a mean of 2.91 targets and the 1,159 clean ones against 2.44 — and both medians are 1.

So neither "it has an alert" nor "it is suspiciously potent" nor "it hits everything" sorts this set by itself. The next turn is where the evidence actually is.

Then take the potent end seriously. Which of the potent ones should I actually distrust, and on what evidence?

🧠 Assistant

The very top of the list is clean, the flagged compounds start just below it — and the decisive evidence turned out to be inside a single paper.

The top is clean

find_ligands_for_target(target_id=2259, max_value_nm=30) and the same call with properties={"exclude_alerts": ["pains"]} return an identical list. Nothing at the top is flagged, so the filter does no work there.

What is up there: Merck's 4-aryl-coumarin FLAP series from 2006 (0.5–4 nM), MK-591, L-746530, and a 2021 Eur. J. Med. Chem. series at 0.75–0.8 nM. Designed, optimised, unflagged. Whatever is wrong with the 5-LOX literature is not at the very top of the potency ranking.

The twelve catechols shift by a median of 20× between the two formats. Zileuton, the approved drug included in the same table as the reference compound, shifts by 1.5× — the smallest ratio of all thirteen rows.

That is the whole argument, and it needs no outside information. In assay A the catechols beat the approved drug by up to 84×. In assay B — same compounds, same hands, same table — they are within 2–3× of it. A compound binding the active site does not much care which of two formats you read it in. A compound whose effect depends on the reducing environment of the assay cares enormously.

And look at 4-phenylcatechol: MW 186, a catechol with one phenyl on it, reported at 50 nM. There is no designed interaction in that structure to explain 50 nM against anything.

NDGA, the textbook case

Nordihydroguaiaretic acid is the natural-product 5-LOX inhibitor of the older literature, and it is a bis-catechol. BindingDB has it from four independent papers:

Sixty-fold across four papers — and a 1991 cell-based EC50 of 330 µM (PMID 1848292), four orders of magnitude away. NDGA is reproducible in the weak sense that several groups all get a number; it is not reproducible in the sense that matters, which is getting the same number.

👤 User

So what do I actually use as the rule?

🧠 Assistant

Use the alert to form a hypothesis and the source count to test it. The alert is a mechanism suggestion, not a score.

The rule

Distrust a compound when all three hold: it is flagged, it is thinly sourced, and it is surprisingly potent for what it is.

WITH best AS (
  SELECT monomerid,
         MIN(value) AS best_nm,
         COUNT(DISTINCT source_id) AS n_sources
  FROM activity
  WHERE polymerid = 2259 AND relation = '=' AND value IS NOT NULL
    AND affinity_type IN ('IC50','Ki','Kd','EC50')
  GROUP BY monomerid)
SELECT COUNT(*)
FROM best b JOIN compound_property p USING (monomerid)
WHERE len(p.pains) > 0        -- flagged
  AND b.n_sources = 1         -- thinly sourced
  AND b.best_nm < 873;        -- beats the approved drug's median

226 compounds — 9% of the set. That is a queue you can actually read, rather than the 416 the alert alone gives you or the 2,411 you started with.

Zileuton is the control, and its spread is instructive

zileuton NDGA
IC50 values 104 4
independent sources 71 4
span 1992–2023 2009–2011
range 100 nM – 100 µM 25 – 1,500 nM
median 873 nM 115 nM

Zileuton's range is wider than NDGA's. Any rule of the form "distrust compounds with scattered values" would throw out the approved drug and keep the catechol.

The difference is not the width of the spread but what it is spread across. Zileuton's 100 nM–100 µM is 71 laboratories over 31 years, with 59 of the 104 values landing between 100 nM and 1 µM. NDGA's 60-fold is four papers. The 2022 catechols' 20-fold is two columns of one table. Breadth of independent agreement is the signal; raw variance is not.

This is also why the potency bands in the first turn were flat. PAINS flags a structure. Whether that structure produced a real number is a property of the evidence, and the evidence lives in source_id, not in the molecule.

One more catch: PAINS misses the drug's actual liability

Zileuton carries no PAINS alert. It carries two Brenk alerts: hydroxamic_acid and Oxygen-nitrogen_single_bond.

The hydroxamic acid is the iron chelator — it is the entire mechanism of the drug — and it is also exactly the group a medicinal chemist would be asked to replace. So exclude_alerts: ["brenk"] on this target deletes the only approved drug in the set, while exclude_alerts: ["pains"] leaves the top of the potency list untouched.

The two catalogues answer different questions:

  • PAINS — is this hit likely an assay artefact? Worth acting on here, because its alerts on 5-LOX name a specific, checkable mechanism.
  • Brenk — is this a group I want in a lead? It matches about a third of all compounds and is not by itself a reason to doubt a measurement.

Neither is a verdict. On this target PAINS earns its keep not as a filter but as a hypothesis generator: it told us to look at redox chemistry, and the two-assay table inside PMID 36201859 is what actually settled it.
