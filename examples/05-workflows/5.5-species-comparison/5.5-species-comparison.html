👤 User

For the dopamine D2 receptor, do compounds bind the human and rat receptors with similar affinity?

🧠 Assistant

Mostly yes. Most compounds bind the human and rat receptors within 10-fold of each other, and the antagonist reference drugs agree within 2-fold. About one compound in seven differs by more than 10-fold.

BindingDB has a separate target for each species: human D2 (UniProt P14416, 17,253 measurements on 10,904 compounds) and rat D2 (P61169, 6,974 measurements on 4,936 compounds). The cow, mouse, monkey and pig receptors are also present, with far less data (586 measurements or fewer). I paired every compound that has an exact ("=") value of the same affinity type on both the human and the rat receptor, took the median per species, and compared the two:

Human and rat D2 are orthologs with a conserved binding site, so close agreement is what you would expect. The exception is the agonist quinpirole, which looks about 6-fold more potent at the rat receptor. Agonist Ki values depend on which receptor state the assay measures (high- or low-affinity, G-protein coupled or not), and rat assays often used brain tissue while human data mostly come from cloned receptors in cell lines. A difference like this one more likely reflects the assay than the species. The agonist pramipexole shows the same direction (7.59 human vs 8.51 rat), but its rat value is a single measurement. A second BindingDB record also named quinpirole (BDBM50018958, a differently drawn structure under the same name) agrees across species (7.15 vs 7.20, with one rat value), so check which record a name points to.

The lesson: when two values differ, first ask whether the assays differ. Comparing orthologs in BindingDB really compares two sets of experiments: different labs, radioligands, tissues and years. Three checks before you read a gap as biology:

  1. Same paper or not. I split the pairs into those where one source measured both species (162 compounds) and those where human and rat values come from different sources (213). The agreement was about the same (mean absolute difference 0.55 vs 0.51 log units), so the spread is not just lab-to-lab noise.
  2. Identical values. 46 of the 375 pairs have exactly the same median, 43 of them from different sources. In the one I checked (BDBM50086852), a 1995 rat paper and a 2000 human paper both report 12 nM. The later paper probably quoted the earlier value rather than measuring it again. (The 1995 rat record also carries no PubMed id in BindingDB, only its title.) Before trusting a perfect match, open both records with get_activity or look at the citations.
  3. Duplicate compound records. Clozapine appears twice (BDBM50001884 and BDBM22869, drawn as different tautomers), and both records agree across species (human/rat 6.97/6.89 and 6.83/6.95). The two records have different InChIKeys (QZUDBNBUXVUHMW vs ZUXABONWMNSFBN), so grouping by InChIKey does not merge them; RDKit's canonical tautomer does (both give CN1CCN(C2=Nc3cc(Cl)ccc3Nc3ccccc32)CC1). For a complete picture, canonicalize tautomers before grouping rather than grouping by BindingDB id.

How this was answered

  • search_targets with the query "DRD2": lists the D2 receptor once per species, each with its own id (human polymerid 5866, rat 3630) and measurement count.
  • run_sql: for each compound, species and affinity type, takes the median exact-relation p_affinity from the activity table and joins human to rat on the compound id, then computes the summary statistics per affinity type.
  • run_sql again: for Ki, the same-source vs different-source split (a source shared by the human and rat medians) and the count of identical pairs; then the full list of 375 Ki pairs (the reference-drug table and the plot), the raw records behind one identical pair, and the two clozapine records.
  • Python (matplotlib, RDKit): draws the scatter plot and checks that the two clozapine records are one tautomer. add_figure adds the plot to this transcript.
